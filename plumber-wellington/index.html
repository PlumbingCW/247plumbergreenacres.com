Before we arrive at your Wellington home

These four moves protect a larger Wellington house, where a single burst line can soak several rooms before anyone notices.

Close the main valve

Find the shutoff where the line enters the house, often near the garage wall or at the front. Turn it clockwise until it stops. A big home can have a second shutoff for the irrigation or pool side, so check both.

Kill power to wet areas

If water is near outlets, a garage freezer or the air handler closet, switch off those breakers. Do not stand in standing water to reach the panel. Stay clear and tell us on the call.

Move things and mop up

Lift rugs, boxes and electronics off wet floors. Tile and wood in open floor plans can wick water far from the source, so towels at the edges help.

Plumbing trouble that shows up in newer Wellington homes

Planned-community housing has its own failure pattern. These are general trends, not a count of any street or builder, so treat them as things to check.

Water heater age

A tank installed when the house was built can be well past its best. Rust-tinted hot water, popping noises or damp around the base are cues to call before it floods the closet. See our water heater replacement page for how that decision works.

PEX supply lines

Many newer homes use PEX tubing. It resists corrosion, but a bad crimp ring, a kinked bend or a fitting near the water heater can drip for weeks inside a wall. A rising water bill with no visible leak points toward leak detection.

Irrigation lines

Big lawns mean long irrigation runs. A cracked riser or a stuck zone valve can waste thousands of gallons and soften the soil near the house. If one zone stays wet when the system is off, shut that zone down and call.

Slow drains in long runs

Large floor plans put bathrooms far from the main line. Grease, wipes and hair gather where pipes run flat for a long stretch, so a single slow tub can mean a partial clog down the line. Drain cleaning usually clears it.
