Four Ways to Repair a Slab Leak in Greenacres, and How to Choose

Once a leak is confirmed under the floor, the real decision is how to fix it. This guide compares the four common repair routes by cost drivers, mess and long-term fit.

By the Greenacres 24/7 Plumbing team.Published October 15, 2026.Updated October 15, 2026.

You have a confirmed leak. Now what are the choices?

After a leak has been located, a homeowner faces four realistic repair routes. One is to open the slab and fix the pipe where it leaks. Another is to abandon the buried pipe and run a new line through walls or the attic. The other two are a full repipe, or a coat of epoxy inside the pipe. Each trades off cost, mess, disruption and how long the fix is likely to last.

The right pick depends on what failed, how many other lines look tired, what is on top of the slab, and what you plan to do with the house. This guide covers the decision only. For how leaks are found, see our slab leak repair page.

Notice that only one of these, the spot repair, touches the slab. Two of the others avoid the slab entirely, which matters if tile, hardwood or a built-in cabinet sits over the leak.

How each route plays out in a real house

Spot repair through the slab

The crew locates the leak with acoustic or thermal tools, marks the spot, and cuts a rectangle of concrete, often a couple of feet on a side. They expose the pipe, replace the damaged length, pressure test, and patch the concrete. It is the least expensive of the four when the leak is isolated, and it fixes the problem right where it started.

The tradeoffs are dust, jackhammer noise and floor finishes. Tile and wood over the cut will not survive, and matching them afterward is part of the cost. If the same pipe has failed once, ask how the rest of that run looks.

Rerouting the line

A reroute leaves the buried pipe in place, capped at both ends, and runs a new line to the fixture through the attic, a wall cavity or a soffit. The slab stays whole, which is why homeowners with finished floors like it. The new pipe is visible and accessible, and a future leak is far easier to find.

The cost is drywall work. Holes are cut to run the line, then patched and painted, and the length of the new route drives the labor. Long routes through finished rooms add up quickly.

Repiping the house

A repipe replaces the supply lines throughout the house with new pipe, typically PEX or copper, run through the attic or walls so nothing new goes under the slab. It makes sense when more than one leak has appeared, or when the pipe material is known to fail in groups. Our repiping page walks through what a whole-house job looks like.

Repair the leak, or repair the reason for the leak

Pinhole leaks often come from the outside in, where a copper pipe rubs the concrete or rock, or from water chemistry attacking thin walls. If you only patch the leak, the next one may be a few feet away.

Ask your plumber what likely caused the failure, and whether the rest of the line shows the same wear. That one answer usually decides between a spot repair and a larger route.

Epoxy lining: a narrow option, not a shortcut

What it does well

  • Avoids cutting the slab or the walls.
  • Can seal pinholes in a pipe that is otherwise sound.
  • Shortens the disruption to a day or so in the right case.

Where it falls short

  • Cannot fix a failed fitting or a split pipe.
  • Needs clean, accessible pipe with proper entry points.
  • Reduces the inside diameter slightly, which matters on small lines.

Whether a lined potable pipe is suitable for drinking water is a question to settle in writing before work starts. Ask what product is used and what approvals it carries, and ask about the permit. We cover the risks in more detail in a separate guide on lining.

Questions to put to any contractor before you decide

Bring this list to the estimate

A good scope answers all of these in writing.

  • Which pipe failed, what is it made of, and how do the neighboring runs look?
  • Will the new line go under the slab, through the attic, or inside walls?
  • What flooring or cabinetry will be removed, and who restores it?
  • Is a permit required, and who pulls it?
  • How will the line be pressure tested before it is closed up?
  • What happens to the old pipe, capped or removed?

Compare answers from two sources if you can. The cheapest bid often leaves out flooring restoration.

Insurance, honestly

Whether your policy covers a slab leak depends on the policy and the cause. Sudden pipe failures and gradual leaks are often treated differently, and some policies exclude the cost of breaking into the slab. Read your policy, call your insurer before work starts, and document the damage with photos.

Local note

Permits and the building department in Greenacres

The City of Greenacres building department accepts permit applications only electronically, and work must be done by a licensed contractor or an exempt owner-builder. For a slab break-in, a reroute or a repipe, ask the building department whether a permit applies and what inspection is needed, because we will not guess at rules we cannot confirm. Inspections are scheduled by 3:00 pm, so ask about timing before you plan the day the floor is open.

Verify the contractor before you sign. Palm Beach County runs a Certificate of Competency search at pbc.gov/pzb, and the state's Verify a Licensee tool is at myfloridalicense.com. A Business Tax Receipt is not a license, and using an unlicensed contractor can bring code enforcement fines to the property owner. Our slab leak repair work is done by a licensed and insured crew.

Whichever repair route you pick, the new piping falls under the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, which Florida builds on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), built on the IPC. Breaking a slab or rerouting lines are the permit-sensitive options.
