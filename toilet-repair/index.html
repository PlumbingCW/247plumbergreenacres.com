Toilet overflowing or won't stop running?

A toilet that is spilling onto the floor is one of the more stressful things to deal with at home, and our 24/7 line is open for exactly that. While you wait for a plumber, reach behind the bowl and turn the small oval or round handle on the supply valve clockwise until it stops. Do not keep flushing, keep cords and power strips off the wet floor, and stay out of any room where water is near an outlet.

If the water will not stop rising, lift the tank lid and push the rubber flapper down into its seat by hand. That cuts off the flow into the bowl in most overflows. When you are ready, call us and tell us what the toilet is doing so we can bring the right parts.

If the shutoff valve will not turn

Old valves in Florida homes often seize because the stem has sat untouched for a decade or more. Do not force it with pliers, since a cracked valve body makes the mess worse. Shut the main valve at the house instead (usually near the water meter at the front of the lot or on an outside wall) and keep everyone off the drains until we arrive.

What the toilet is trying to tell you

Callers tend to describe the same handful of problems, and each one points at a different part. Here is how we read them.

Running or hissing after the flush

Water keeps trickling into the bowl or the tank refills every few minutes by itself. Usually a worn flapper that no longer seals, or a fill valve that will not shut off.

Weak or half flush

The bowl swirls but does not clear. Mineral buildup in the rim holes, a chain that is too long or short, or a partial clog in the trap can each cause it.

Water at the base

A damp ring or pooling around the foot of the toilet often means the wax ring has failed or the closet bolts have loosened. Check that it is not tank condensation dripping down the outside.

Rocking on the floor

Even a slight wobble breaks the seal on the wax ring over time. The cause can be loose bolts, an uneven floor, or flange rot underneath.

Gurgling or bubbling

A toilet that gurgles when the shower or washer drains is often venting a bigger problem downstream. Two toilets acting up at once usually points to the main line.

Clogged and not clearing

One plunge that works is a simple clog. A bowl that refills to the brim again within minutes, even after plunging, suggests something deeper in the line.

Most of these are same-day repairs, and when you call after hours we can usually line up a visit for the next opening, depending on schedule.

The parts inside the tank, in plain terms

Lift the lid and you will see four things that matter. The fill valve is the tall tower on the left that refills the tank and the bowl. The flapper is the rubber stopper at the bottom that lifts when you flush, and the flush valve is the large drain opening it sits on. The chain connects the handle lever to the flapper.

Flapper and fill valve

These are the most common culprits for a toilet that runs. A flapper hardens, warps or collects grit and lets water seep past, and a fill valve can wear out or get stuck so it never shuts off. Both are inexpensive parts, and replacing them is usually a short visit.

Wax ring and flange

The wax ring is a thick gasket that seals the toilet to the drain pipe in the floor. When it fails you get a slow leak around the base, sometimes with a faint sewage smell. The flange is the ring the toilet bolts to, and if it is cracked or sits below the floor surface, a new wax ring alone will not hold.

Installing a new toilet

A new install starts with shutting off the supply, draining the tank and lifting the old toilet off its bolts. We scrape the old wax, inspect the flange and the floor around it, then set a new wax ring and bowl, snug the bolts evenly so the china does not crack, and connect a fresh supply line.

Before you buy, measure the rough-in: the distance from the wall to the center of the floor drain. Most are 12 inches, but 10 and 14 exist, and a mismatch means the toilet will not sit right. Also decide between round and elongated bowls, since elongated ones need more room in front. If the home uses a washlet or bidet seat, mention it, because the supply and a nearby outlet matter.

What insurance may and may not cover

Homeowners policies treat sudden water damage differently from slow leaks, and many do not pay to replace a worn fixture itself. If an overflow soaked drywall or flooring, photograph it, stop the water, and call your insurer to ask what your policy covers.

When the toilet keeps backing up

If plunging only helps for a day, the clog is probably not in the toilet at all. A drain cleaning visit with a cable can clear the branch line behind it, and a camera look tells us whether roots or a belly in the pipe are the real cause.

When two or more fixtures back up together, or the shower gurgles when you flush, the trouble is further out. That calls for sewer line cleaning on the main run to the street. Please stop flushing until it is cleared, because every flush adds to what ends up on the floor.

Toilets, fixtures and the code

Fixture installation is covered by the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, which Florida builds on the International Plumbing Code (IPC), built on the IPC. Replacing a toilet means setting it, sealing it and connecting the supply to that standard.

Repairs such as a flapper or fill valve are routine maintenance. A new toilet or moved drain is a bigger job, so ask the building department when in doubt.
