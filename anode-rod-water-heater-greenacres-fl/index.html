Water Heater Anode Rod in Greenacres: What It Does and When to Replace It

A metal rod inside your tank quietly takes the corrosion so the steel does not. Here is how to check it and when to let it go.

By the Greenacres 24/7 Plumbing team.Published October 15, 2026.Updated October 15, 2026.

What the rod does inside the tank

The anode rod is a long metal rod, usually magnesium or aluminum, screwed into the top of a tank water heater. It corrodes on purpose. Because it is more reactive than the steel tank, it takes the attack from the water and protects the shell, which is why it is called a sacrificial anode.

When the rod is used up, the water starts working on the tank wall. A heater with a good rod can outlast one with a spent rod by a wide margin. Checking it takes a little time and the right socket, and replacing it costs far less than a new heater.

If you suspect yours is gone or the tank is already leaking, a water heater repair visit can inspect it and tell you whether the tank is worth saving.

How to inspect an anode rod in plain steps

This is a job for someone comfortable with tools. If the tank is old or the rod is stuck, call a plumber instead of forcing it.

Turn off power or gas and the cold supply

Switch off the breaker for an electric unit or set the gas control to off. Close the cold water valve on top of the heater.

Relieve pressure and drain a few gallons

Open a hot tap in the house and drain some water from the tank drain valve so the level drops below the rod's port.

Loosen the rod

The hex head sits on top of the tank. A breaker bar and a 1-1/16 inch socket are common. Use steady force. A rod that will not budge may be seized in the fitting.

Pull it and look

A healthy rod is thick with a little roughness. One that is pencil-thin in places, or shows bare steel wire, is spent. Replace it, then refill the tank and check for leaks before power or gas comes back.

Clearance is the usual obstacle. Some heaters sit under a low ceiling and cannot take a rigid rod. Flexible, segmented rods exist for those spots.

Smell and symptom: anode rod or something else

Rotten-egg odor in hot water is the classic sign people connect to the rod, but it is not the only cause.

When replacing the rod is not worth it

A new rod only helps if the tank is still sound. If the heater leaks from the bottom or seams, if rust-colored water has been showing up for a while, or if the tank is well past the ten-year mark with a rod that has not been touched, the shell may already be thin.

Another sign that you are too late is a rod so corroded that it crumbles in your hand or leaves a pile of flakes in the drain water. Even then, checking is not wasted effort. You learn the tank's condition and can plan a water heater replacement on your schedule instead of in a flood.

Cases where a new rod makes sense

A heater a few seasons old with no leaks, mild odor and a rod nearly gone is a good candidate. So is a unit in a home where a leak would do real damage and you want to stretch its life while you plan.
